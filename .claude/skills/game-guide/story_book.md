## Chapter One — What the River Carries

The storm had been the worst of Ares's life.

He had been through bad ones before — the kind that came in off the deep forest and rattled the shutters and made the animals restless through the night. This was not that kind. This was the kind that made you wonder, somewhere in the small hours, whether the morning would come. Lightning that hit close enough to feel in the walls. Thunder that arrived with no delay, no space between the light and the sound. The kind of storm that did not feel like weather. That felt like something had decided.

By the time it passed, the sky had gone the pale gray of early morning and Vinemore was lying in its aftermath. He could hear it before he opened the door — the sounds a village makes when it is assessing damage. Voices calling across yards. Something being dragged. A roof tile somewhere shattering on a path.

He was at the stove, still in the clothes he had worn all night — sleep had not happened in any meaningful way — when Mark knocked.

Mark lived next door. He was younger than Ares by a few years, old enough that they had been friends since Ares could remember, young enough that there was still something quick and unguarded about the way he moved through the world. He knocked the way he always knocked, which was like he was asking permission.

"Mr. Leman." He said it fast, the way you say something when the weight of it has not yet caught up with the words. "You know — with the health problems. He's the fourth one now."

Ares set down the pan.

Mark was already explaining that he was going to find Peter, the doctor. Already half-turned to go. The urgency in him was the kind that comes from not knowing what to do with information, only knowing it needs to be somewhere useful. He left before Ares could ask anything.

Four people sick. Same symptoms. Ares had heard about the first two from people in the square over the past few days. The third — Miria, the woman who was married to Dario — only yesterday. And now Leman, who had been managing his own health for years before any of this, who everyone in the village knew by the particular careful way he moved through public spaces, as though conserving himself.

Ares put on his coat and went out into the broken morning.

---

The village looked like it had argued with the night and not quite won.

Fences down. A section of thatch from someone's roof heaped in the road. Two men he recognized from the eastern end of the lane were working to clear a fallen branch from in front of their gate, talking to each other with the compressed efficiency of people who have a lot to do before the day can properly start. A woman was collecting clay tiles from her yard, setting them in a pile with the concentrated expression of someone doing arithmetic in her head.

He walked through it toward the houses he knew.

---

The old woman's house was on the quieter edge of the village, where the trees came closest and the forest sounds bled into the morning air. Everyone called her the old woman who lived alone. He had never heard anyone use a given name for her — there was one, probably, somewhere in the village's memory, but it had been replaced long ago by the description, which was accurate and was enough.

He knocked. Waited. Knocked again.

The door was locked and nothing answered from inside. Somewhere behind it a woman was sick in a way that had already brought down three other people, and she had no one to answer the door on her behalf.

He stood there a moment, looking at the wood. Then he moved on.

---

Dario was standing outside his own house with the posture of a man who has appointed himself the last line of defense between his wife and the rest of the world.

"She's exhausted," he said. "She needs rest."

From inside, Miria's voice — thin, deliberate, carrying the effort of someone who has decided they are going to be useful: "I'm awake. Let him in."

Dario held the door a moment longer, as though the decision might still reverse itself. Then he stepped aside.

She was in bed, pale in the particular way of the sick, something drawn thin. She talked carefully. The days before the fever — where she had gone, what she had eaten, what had felt different. She did not know what had caused it. None of it resolved into a clear cause. Dario, sitting beside her with the look of someone who needs a theory to hold, offered the one he had: "The old woman — she was the first, you know. They found her so weak. Maybe it was something they all ate. Something small that didn't agree with certain people."

He said it the way people say things they want to be true.

Neither of them said anything about the pregnancy. Ares did not know about it yet.

---

Mr. Gragi opened the third door and looked Ares over with careful eyes.

"Are you a doctor?"

"No," Ares said. "But I'm trying to understand what's happening."

Gragi considered this. Then he stepped back.

Derol was propped against pillows in the back room, with the look of a man who was accustomed to moving fast and found the stillness of illness deeply irritating. He and his son Fredy had been at the eastern river — just playing near the water, the way they sometimes did, nothing purposeful. Before they turned for home the fever came on sudden and complete, the way a door closes. He almost went down in the road. Fredy called out. Vincent had been passing and helped carry him home.

---

Vincent was in the square, easy to find. He confirmed it without being asked twice: right beside the river, right before they turned for home. He had been close enough to catch Derol before he hit the ground. Helped bring him home himself.

Then, while Ares was taking that in: "It's a spot I know, actually. The old woman — the one who lives alone — she goes there nearly every other morning. Mushrooms, greens, the kind of things that grow along the bank. You can find her there most mornings if you know where to look."

He said it as information, nothing more. He did not say it as a clue. Ares did not take it as one. But he filed it somewhere.

The square was the center of Vinemore the way squares always are — surrounded by the things that make a place function. And in one corner, on a plinth of pale stone that had been there since before Ares was born, the statue.

The name: Asotos. The inscription described a night long ago when thieves came to Vinemore in the dark, and a man who stood against them, and a battle that ended in a victory that cost everything. A poisoned knife. A morning he did not wake to. The statue had been there so long it had become part of the square's furniture, something you stopped seeing through familiarity.

Ares looked at it for a moment — longer than usual. Then he went east.

---

Daedalos was at the east path the way a stone sits in a wall.

"Not without a weapon," he said, before Ares had finished his first sentence. "Not these days. Something's wrong in those woods and I'm not going to stand here and send people into it unprepared."

He mentioned Pontos — used to carry a good wooden sword, though he hadn't seen it on him lately. Mr. Kardino near the square had a magical staff of some kind. The shop, Mr. Lulu, might have something. Nathan the blacksmith might too.

Then he shifted his spear — not a gesture, just a habit, the way a craftsman adjusts a tool they've been holding for hours. He looked at it with something approaching affection. "Me — I like this one. Close enough to do real work, far enough that I'm not pressed against whatever I'm dealing with. Not quite arm's reach, not quite a throw. There's a distance that's right for things, and this finds it." He had called her Mariane for so long he had forgotten when he'd started.

Ares turned back toward the village with two directions in mind: a weapon, and another conversation with Miria and Dario. The guard had said they might know more.

---

He went to the couple first.

Something had shifted since the morning. Dario opened the door without the defended posture of before, and Miria sat up straighter.

She was pregnant.

She said it plainly, without preparing for it. They had told no one — it was early, and it was theirs, and they had wanted to keep it that way a little longer. But four people were ill, a doctor was being summoned, the village was already coming apart at the edges from the storm, and she could not hold something that might be relevant to understanding what was happening. The pregnancy explained her fragility. She had been the third to fall ill and she did not think it was coincidence.

They were sitting with this when the knock came.

Peter walked in. He was the village doctor, a practical man with the particular tiredness of someone who had been carrying too much information for too many days. He had just come from Leman — Mark's father's close friend, the man with the long-standing health problems — and the severity there had worried him enough that he had stopped here before going home.

When Miria told him about the pregnancy he let out a breath that was not quite relief. Close enough to it that it mattered, but not enough to relax.

He sat and listened. Together in that room, all four cases at once:

The old woman — age, alone, a body with fewer reserves. And she goes to the river bank herself, Peter said, nearly every other morning. Collects greens and mushrooms from the edge. She has done it for years.
Derol — beside the river, in the water.
Miria — pregnant, her body working in two directions at once.
Leman — years of managing something chronic, a system already carrying more than it should.

Same symptoms across all of them. The dizziness that arrived without warning. The fatigue that did not respond to sleep. The heart moving too fast. Peter worked through it aloud the way doctors think — systematically, eliminating, following the thread. Not food; they didn't share meals with any regularity. Not the air. But the river: Derol had been in it directly, the others had been drinking from the village water that came from the east, from the same source.

He stood. He was going to call a meeting. The whole village. Tonight. He left with the weight of someone who understands that what they are about to do will change the mood of the place, and is doing it anyway.

Ares stood in the doorway and watched him go and thought about where the river came from.

East. Through the farming woods and the bridge road and past the old abandoned mines — empty for years now, the entrance blocked after the timbers rotted and the last of the workers left and an order came from Aethion to close them. The cave at the mines: not sealed, just blocked. A barrier to keep people from wandering in. The river came out from inside it.

If the water was wrong, the beginning of the wrong was in there.

Between here and there: a guard who needed a weapon, and now the need for a weapon, and whatever the farming woods would ask of him before the bridge. He went to find what he needed.

---

He went north first, into the edge of the farming woods, following what Daedalos had said about Pontos. The morning light came through the canopy in the way it does after a storm — cleaner than usual, the air washed down to something almost sharp. The rats in the undergrowth had their opinions about his presence and expressed them. He moved carefully, keeping distance from anything that wanted to test him, watching the ground more than the path ahead.

He found the sword in a hollow at the base of an old oak, off the main path, in a place you would only find if you were looking or were the kind of person who looked down rather than forward. Wooden, well-made, wrapped in damp cloth. Sitting there like it had been waiting for someone to notice it.

He picked it up. The weight told him someone had made it with care.

He thought: *this belongs to someone.*

---

Pontos was not difficult to find. A man in his fifties, with hands that had worked his whole life and a face that had been missing something for a while. When Ares held the sword out, his expression went through several things quickly.

"Where did you find it?"

Ares told him.

Pontos held it the way you hold something you had already half-grieved. He thanked Ares with the real kind of thanks — the kind that costs something to say — and went inside and came back with a folded jacket. Worn but well-made, reinforced at the seams. Adventurer's clothes. Better protection than what Ares had on.

Then the deal: creature parts from the north woods, in exchange for borrowing the sword. Pontos needed them; Ares needed a weapon for the east gate. Simple.

He went back to the farming woods, more deliberately this time, with the sword in hand and the math working differently. The rats lost some of their confidence. He came back with what Pontos had asked for and handed it over and held up his end of things.

Pontos took the parts, passed over the sword, and then paused. The kind of pause that is actually an afterthought with weight to it.

"Is the sword comfortable for you?" He turned it slightly, looking at it rather than Ares. "Only because if it's not — if you wanted something with a different feel — Lulu might have a dagger at the shop. And Nathan the blacksmith has a hand axe he's mentioned before."

Ares thought about that. He thought about Mariane and the right distance and the different ways of solving things. He handed the sword back to Pontos and said he would return for it if he needed to.

---

Nathan's forge smelled of iron and effort. Nathan was a large, direct man who listened without interrupting. When Ares was done explaining — the river, the patients, the cave to the east, the guard who needed a reason to step aside — Nathan went to the corner and came back with the hand axe.

"I was going to put this to work this week," he said. "But this sounds like it matters more." He turned it once in his hands and passed it over. "Bring it back when you're done."

The weight of it felt right. Different from the sword — committed in a way the sword was not. Each swing would be deliberate. He thought: *yes. This.*

---

Daedalos looked at the axe and nodded and moved aside. No ceremony. The east path opened.

---

The farming woods were easier with a proper weapon. He moved through the north section without stopping, following the paths he had walked enough times now to know them, and came out the other side into the bridge road.

Kadmios was at the break in the bridge.

The carpenter was a solid man — steady in the way of someone who builds things for a living and trusts that problems can be approached if you find the right angle. He had been standing here since early morning, since before the village was fully awake, because the bridge was his problem in the same way the east road was his road and the carriage from Roteltree was his carriage. The storm had taken something that was supposed to be reliable and he had been looking at the damage ever since.

When Ares approached, Kadmios did not waste time.

"I know why you're here," he said. "The water. Peter's been talking." He looked at the fallen trunk across the bridge span — lightning had hit the oak on the bank above, the tree had come straight down across the bridge, exactly wrong. "The road's blocked on my end too. I was expecting a carriage from Roteltree."

Then, without a change in tone: "Lesen came to me this morning. Her twins didn't come home last night."

He said it the way you say something that is both new and old at the same time. Because he had heard almost those exact words before — twenty years ago, when Lesen had come to him and said that Terin had not come home from the mines. He had known Terin the way you know someone you have worked beside for your whole life — since before either of them had a profession, when they were both young men who went where the mine went and did what the mine asked. When Terin had started going back to the closed tunnels alone, Kadmios had not known. Only Lesen had known. And when she came to him and told him everything, he had assembled whoever he could find and gone in.

He had found Terin at the bottom of a gap. A long fall in the dark. The kind of end that a man who knew every passage could still not always prevent.

He did not tell all of this to Ares. Some of it was in the telling; the rest was in the way he stood looking at the damaged bridge as if it were personally responsible for the problem.

"The twins were Kardino's students," he said. "Both of them. They were — " he paused, looking for the right word, then settling for the accurate one — "gifted. The kind of gifted that makes you take notice. Each of them working a different element. They'd been paying attention to this disruption for weeks. Whether they went out into that storm because of it, or whether it was something else, I don't know."

He was quiet for a moment.

"My son came back yesterday evening from the south forest. A hunt — he's been out there a few days, he's tired. But this is more than one person's problem." He looked at Ares directly. "I'll send him to meet you on the road. He knows the east path well enough. You shouldn't go into that cave alone."

---

They moved the trunk together — Ares, Kadmios, Pontos who had walked down the road without being asked, and Nathan, who arrived with his sleeves already rolled.

It was not elegant work. The trunk had weight and settled weight and the stubbornness of something that had been sitting in place all morning developing opinions about staying. Kadmios found the angles. Nathan provided the argument for moving past them. They worked in stages, in the silence of physical effort, wood grinding on old stone, until it was done.

And then it was clear.

The bridge held. The river ran under it, low and cloudy and wrong-smelling, moving west toward the village that was drinking it.

Kadmios put his hands on the railing and tested it and straightened up without saying anything, because there was nothing that needed saying. He would go back to his shop now. There was work to do, and the carriage from Roteltree might still come if the road was passable by midday.

Nathan rolled his sleeves back down and looked at the bridge with the expression of a man who has done a job and is satisfied with the result.

Pontos told Ares to be careful in there, and meant it.

---

Ares crossed the bridge.

On the other side the path continued east, into the thicker trees, toward the rise where the old mines had been. The river ran beside him, moving against the direction he was walking, carrying whatever it had picked up somewhere in the dark ahead of him.

He was looking for two things now: whatever was wrong with the water, and two young people who had gone out into a storm and not come back. Whether those were one problem or two he did not yet know.

Somewhere on the road ahead, Kadmios's son was making his way toward him. Tired from a hunt, willing to do this anyway, because his father had asked.

Ares kept walking. The morning was burning off into something that was going to become a clear day — the kind that comes after a very bad night, almost too bright, the world insisting on itself. He followed the river east, toward where it started.

---

*End of Chapter One.*

## Chapter Two — The Dark Below

He found Mike at the edge of the tree line, just past the bridge, leaning against an oak with the posture of someone who had been walking since before he was properly awake.

He was younger than Ares had expected — early twenties, with the build of someone who spent most of his time outdoors, and a tiredness in him that was specifically the tiredness of a long hunt followed by not enough sleep followed by a father who said *this is more important than resting*. He had a short blade at his hip and a look on his face that said he had already decided this was the right thing to do and was going to stop being annoyed about it sometime in the next hour.

"My father gave me the shape of it," he said. "Sick people, bad water, twins who went missing. You're the one who put it all together?"

"I had help," Ares said.

Mike considered this, seemed to find it acceptable, and fell into step beside him.

---

The road east ran between the trees with the river on one side and old forest on the other. The smell of the water was wrong in a way that was hard to name — not rot, not exactly, more like something that was meant to move had stopped moving in places it shouldn't. The wrong smell of stillness.

Mike did not fill the quiet with talking. That suited Ares. They moved in the way of two people who do not yet know each other well enough to speak comfortably, which meant they said only what needed saying, which was enough.

An hour into the road, slightly south of the main track, Kardino had mentioned a shrine.

The Verdant Temple was small — not a building so much as an arrangement of shaped stone around a natural clearing, old enough that the forest had grown into it rather than around it. Roots had found the seams between the stones and stayed there. Moss had made itself at home. The whole thing looked like the forest had been keeping it, rather than the other way around.

The Resonance in the place sat differently than the woods around it. Quieter. More specific. Like a conversation happening at a register too low to hear but not too low to feel.

Ares sat down at the center of it and ate what he had brought. He was not a religious man in any formal sense. He did not know the correct words or the correct posture. He just sat and ate and thought about two fifteen-year-olds who had gone out into the worst storm of his lifetime and had not come back.

He thought: *let them be in there. Alive. Waiting.*

It was not exactly a prayer. It was more like wanting something hard enough that the wanting needed somewhere to go.

Mike sat at the edge of the clearing and did not come in, out of respect for something he could feel even if he could not have named it. He ate too. Neither of them said anything.

Then they got up and walked toward the mines.

---

The entrance to the old mine was visible from a distance. The barrier that had been built across it — timber framing and packed earth, meant to communicate *no further* rather than actually stop anything determined — had been breached. Not broken down. Moved, carefully, from the inside, and left off to one side in a way that suggested someone who knew what they were doing and was not in a hurry.

Beside it, twenty feet to the south, the river came out of the hillside through a separate opening. The cave there was natural, lower, threaded through with the sound of moving water. Two entrances. Two systems that shared the same hill.

Mike looked at both of them.

"River or mine?" he said.

The river was the water source. The mine was the twins' most likely path — they would have known these tunnels, or known *of* them. Ares thought about the twins spending weeks watching the disruption. Trying to understand it. He thought about Kardino's face when he said *they were paying attention.*

"They went into the mine," Ares said. "That's where I'll go. You take the river."

Mike nodded. He looked at the river entrance for a moment the way you look at something you are about to commit to, then turned back.

"If you find the way through before I do, come find me," he said. "Don't go all the way in alone."

"Same to you," Ares said.

They went in as night fell around them.

---

The first rooms of the old mine still had light.

Three lanterns, oil-fed, burning on their brackets as if someone had set them out that morning. The twins. They had come in here prepared, which meant they had known they were coming, which meant the storm had not surprised them, not entirely. They had gone out into it with intention.

The galleries beyond were old — timber-framed ceilings, channels cut into the floor where drainage had once run, the iron brackets where ore carts had moved along their tracks. The smell was stone and cold and the particular damp of sealed spaces. Ares moved carefully, hand axe ready, lantern taken from the wall.

He found the evidence of the fight before he found anything else.

Scorch marks on the stone in patterns that were not natural — too precise, too concentrated. Ice crystals at the edges of a gallery junction, not melting, sitting in the cold air as if whatever had made them had not finished its business. Creatures that should have been sleeping in the deep rock lying where they had been thrown, not by him and not by anything physical — by force of a different kind, applied in a way that left nothing visible except the result.

The twins had come through here. They had fought something — or cleared something — or both, in the process of reaching whatever they had come for.

He followed the trail of their work.

---

The cave-in was a wall of rubble across the main gallery.

Not a collapse. A collapse has a shape that comes from weight and accident — the way things fall when they are no longer held. This had a different shape. It had been made. The support timbers had been cracked at specific points; the fall had been controlled, directed. Someone had looked at the structure of this tunnel and known exactly where to push.

The twins. Running from something, burning their remaining strength on a door that would slow it behind them.

Ares stood in front of it and understood that whatever was on the other side of that rubble was what had driven two gifted teenagers to seal it in with them.

He did not try to move it. Not alone. Not yet.

He turned back the way he had come and started looking for the wall.

---

Kardino had said: *the two systems are not entirely separate.* He had said it in the way you say something that is technically true but carries more weight than it sounds like.

Ares found the crack after twenty minutes of moving slowly along the shared wall with the lantern close. It was not a gap. It was a fracture — natural, old, running through the rock where two geological things had once settled against each other and not quite agreed. Just wide enough. Just.

He took the axe from his back, exhaled, and went through.

---

The river cave was everything the mine was not.

The mine had been cut by human hands with a purpose. The river cave had been cut by water over ten thousand years with patience. The river ran through the center of it in a channel worn smooth, and the ceiling ranged from low to unreachable, and the formations grew where they wanted. Wet stone caught the lantern light in ways that made the room seem larger than it was, then smaller, then uncertain.

He heard the voices before he saw the light.

---

Mike was in the safe room — a wider section of the cave where the rock made a natural shelf and the floor was dry. He was sitting against the wall with a cloth wrapped tight around his left shoulder, the other end held by a boy who was doing a careful job with it and watching the entrance at the same time.

Felan.

He was smaller than Ares had imagined from what Kardino had said — fifteen, slight, with the specific stillness of someone who had been through something bad enough that their body had decided to become quiet rather than show it. His hands were steady. His eyes when they found Ares were the eyes of someone who had been waiting for exactly this.

"You're from the village," he said. Not a question.

"Yes."

He finished the bandage and stood.

Mike, for his part, looked at Ares with the flat expression of a man who had been expecting a wall and had found a door instead.

"Spider," he said, by way of explanation for the shoulder. "Not the small kind."

"I gathered," Ares said. He crouched down beside him, checked the work Felan had done — good work, careful — and straightened up. "Can you move?"

"I can move. I'm not fast." He glanced at the passage. "What did you find on the mine side?"

"Cave-in. Deliberate. Something is on the other side of it." Ares looked at Felan. "What is on the other side of it?"

Felan sat. He looked at his hands for a moment, in the way of someone organizing things.

Then he told them.

---

There was a wanderer, he said. A man — or something that looked like a man, or was close enough to one that the distinction didn't matter for the purposes of a conversation. He had come to them three weeks ago. Not through any official channel, not through Kardino or the school — he had found them directly, in the woods where they went to practice, because he could feel what they were doing from a distance and he knew what it meant.

He had explained, carefully and without drama, what was happening to Vinemore's forest.

The disruption in the Resonance was not accidental. Someone had made it. A race — old, long-acting, not from this part of the world — had been placing disruption wells in areas they intended to clear. It was how they worked: poison the land gradually, make it unlivable, move the existing inhabitants out through sickness and fear and ecological collapse, then move in once the place was empty. Vinemore was one of many. The well in the mine complex had been placed and maintained for months.

The wanderer had been fighting this expansion for a long time. Long enough that fighting was not the right word anymore — surviving it, slowing it, building barriers at the edges of places that had not yet been lost. The barriers cost him. Every one cost him something he could not get back. He was running out.

He had come to Felan and Vinie because they were the most powerful young Resonance users in a hundred miles and they were sitting in the middle of a place that was about to be taken. He had asked them to help him build a barrier around the disruption well before it fully stabilized.

They had agreed.

The storm was not weather. The night it hit, they went to the Verdant Temple to begin the ritual. Something in the consuming race's presence in the cave had sensed what they were doing — or someone had. The ritual was broken before it was complete. The three of them ran. Into the mine, because the twins knew it and the wanderer could follow their lead, and because the consuming race member that maintained the well would be slower in tunnels than in open forest.

They split in the dark. Vinie went deeper. Felan and the wanderer fought their way to a gallery junction and turned the timbers with what they had left. The wanderer was badly wounded. He pressed something into Felan's hands — Felan held up his palm, showed nothing there now, showed the memory of something having been there — and told him to survive, and went.

Felan had not seen him since.

"The well," Ares said. "Where is it?"

"Deep mine. Past the cave-in. There's a chamber at the water source — the crack is in the floor there."

"And Vinie?"

Felan's face did something complicated. "She went into the river side. Looking for a second way around. I lost track of her when the tunnels split."

Ares and Mike looked at each other.

"Then we go deeper," Ares said.

---

Mike stayed. His shoulder was not going to get better by moving through spider territory and Felan, worn down as he was, was worth more in the mine than in the river cave. They would wait in the safe room.

Ares went in alone.

---

The Spider Queen had built herself a kingdom in the deepest section of the river cave, where the ceiling rose high enough to make a proper vaulted space and the webbing was thick enough to change the texture of the room. It sat in the center of it: large, wrong, a thing that had been drawn here by the energy bleeding up through the rock and had made itself comfortable in the way of an animal that has found a place where nothing challenges it.

Vinie was at the far end of the room, wrapped in web, suspended off the floor. Alive. Her breathing visible. Her eyes closed.

The Spider Queen did not let Ares get to her without argument.

The fight was long and ugly and several times felt like it was going to go badly. The Queen was fast in ways that her size should not have allowed and had a particular patience — she would retreat to the edge of her web, let Ares extend himself into the middle of the room, and then come back from a direction he had not expected. He learned her. He paid for the learning in scrapes and impacts and one moment where only the Adventurer's jacket between him and a set of legs that hit like hammers kept the outcome from being different.

He damaged her. Methodically. Took what she offered and gave as much back as she gave him, until she was moving differently — slower, favoring one side. Until the calculation changed.

She went up.

The ceiling had a break in it, a large fracture in the rock running across the vault, and she went through it fast and without looking back, the sound of her retreating into some upper passage until there was only silence and the slow drip of water and Ares standing in the center of a room that now had no queen.

He found the blasting powder in a pile of debris she had collected along the far wall — the collected miscellany of years of habitation, tools from wherever the water had carried things, old equipment from the mine that had drifted through the system. The cylinder of packed powder sat among it as if it had been waiting for him to need it.

He cut Vinie free.

---

She was lighter than he expected. Lighter and colder. He carried her back through the river cave, through the tunnels, back toward the safe room and the lantern light and the sound of other people. She woke somewhere in the middle of it — not alert, not able to speak, but awake in the way of someone returning to a body that has been somewhere else for a while.

She looked at him. He could not read the look. He kept moving.

---

The four of them sat in the safe room with the lantern between them.

Mike took one look at Vinie and handed over his remaining clean cloth without being asked. Felan held his sister's hands and talked to her quietly in a way that was not entirely about the content of the words. She held his hands back. She had not spoken yet.

They ate what was left of what Ares had brought. Then Felan said: "The cave-in. Can you break it?"

Ares set the blasting powder on the shelf between them.

Felan looked at it. Then he looked at his own hands — and something in him made a decision. "I can help with what's on the other side," he said. "Whatever it is. I'm not done."

"You don't have to," Ares said.

"My mother is in that village," he said. "And your village is drinking that water."

Ares did not argue.

Mike would stay with Vinie. If something came into the river cave from outside, he would deal with it on one arm. He said this as though it were not complicated.

"Don't do anything dramatic," Felan said to him.

"I'm the one that got stabbed," Mike said. "You go deal with the terrible creature in the mine."

---

The cave-in gave under the blasting powder and Felan's careful application of force in the exact right direction. It did not so much collapse as *release* — the rubble moving with the particular suddenness of things that have been held past their natural moment.

The gallery beyond was cold in a way the rest of the mine had not been. A dry cold. A specific cold. The lantern flame moved in a way it should not have, pulled toward the depths, as if the air down there was being consumed by something that wanted it.

They went in.

---

It knew they were coming.

The creature was in the chamber before the well — a wider space where the ore veins had converged and the old miners had carved out extra room for the work. It was not large. It was not monstrous in the way of the Spider Queen. It was roughly the size of a person and moved with the careful economy of something that had been thinking about every step for a very long time.

When they came through the gallery entrance it looked at them and did not attack immediately.

For a moment — a strange, suspended moment — it appeared to be considering whether there was something to say.

Then it decided there wasn't, or decided it didn't matter, or decided that talking was something that happened under different circumstances, and the fight began.

It was a different kind of fight from the Spider Queen. The Queen had been animal intelligence — fast, adaptive, territorial. This was something with more planning in it. It used the room deliberately. It pulled on the energy coming up through the floor in ways that Felan could feel and tried to counter, the two of them locked in a kind of running argument conducted at a register Ares could not access. Ares kept it physical — kept it from getting distance, kept it from settling, gave Felan the room to do what he was doing.

It took both of them. Neither of them alone would have managed it.

When it reached the point where it was going to lose, it stopped.

It looked at Ares. It looked at Felan. The look was not animal, was not panicked. It was the specific look of a decision being made and a calculation being reached.

"I will return," it said, in something that was not quite language but arranged itself into words by the time it arrived. "With help."

Then it went through the crack in the floor.

The crack that was the well. The source.

---

The chamber on the far side of the fight room was small and old and had not been touched by human hands since the mine was built around it. The floor was natural stone, uncut, and in the center of it the crack ran from wall to wall — a foot wide, old, the kind of feature that looked geological until you understood what was moving through it.

The energy came up in a way that was visible if you knew how to look. Felan stood at the edge of it for a long time without saying anything. Then he said, very quietly: "I don't know how to close this."

Ares had expected that. He crouched down beside the crack, held the lantern over it, and looked in. The light went a little way and then the dark swallowed it, and what came back was not cold or heat or any physical thing, just the sense of something very large moving very slowly somewhere below.

"Neither do I," he said.

They stood there in the way you stand in front of something that is bigger than what you came prepared for.

"The water will start to clear," Felan said. "Without it being maintained. Slowly. But it will."

"How slowly?"

"Weeks. Possibly longer." He looked at the crack as if looking at it long enough might change it. "We did not solve it. We interrupted it."

"It said it would come back."

"Yes."

Ares straightened up. There was nothing more to do here. No mechanism, no lever, no solution he could build with an axe and two hands and borrowed time. Whatever this was, it was a problem for a different conversation — one that required understanding he did not yet have.

He picked up the lantern.

They left the well behind.

---

They came out of the cave four of them: Ares with the axe and the blasting powder and the particular exhaustion that comes from a night of the wrong kind of work. Mike moving carefully around a shoulder that was going to remind him of this for weeks. Felan walking beside his sister, one hand on her arm, both of them quiet in the way of people who have not yet processed what has happened to them and are wise enough to know that is not tonight's work.

The eastern sky was beginning to separate colors. Gray from darker gray. The hour before dawn.

They had been in the cave all night.

---

The road back was wrong from the first mile.

The animals came out of the undergrowth in ways that animals do not — without the normal caution, without the reading of threat, as if some signal that ordinarily kept them in their patterns had been turned off. A deer walked directly across the path in front of Ares and stopped there and looked at him without running. A group of birds moved from a tree to the ground and then back without the usual logic. The forest felt like a room that had been rearranged while you were sleeping and everything was in the wrong place.

Nothing attacked. But none of it was right.

Mike kept himself between Vinie and the undergrowth, one-armed, watching. Felan walked with one hand extended slightly, as if he were feeling the air.

"The disruption," he said, answering a question Ares had not asked. "It's worse than it was last night. Being unmaintained doesn't make it quiet — it makes it louder. Like removing pressure from a pipe."

"For how long?"

"Until it shrinks. Or until something else happens."

They did not run. Running with two people who had been through what the twins had been through was not a choice. They moved fast and stayed together and watched and kept moving.

---

Vinemore's eastern gate was dark.

Not the comfortable dark of a village in early morning — the wrong dark, the kind that makes the hairs on your arms move independently and your body start thinking before your mind catches up. At the edge of the tree line Ares stopped and held up a hand and they all stopped.

Three of them. Visible even in the gray light. Things that had the rough shapes of creatures but did not move like creatures, did not move like anything Ares could name with confidence. The shape that was wrong and the movement that was wrong and the feeling in the air around them that was the same feeling as the crack in the well's chamber, just distributed.

From the gate, a voice — low and urgent: "Stay back. Move slowly."

Daedalos.

He had his spear leveled and the particular stillness of someone who has already made every decision they need to make and is waiting for the right moment.

Three against one. Until it wasn't.

Ares moved in from the east at the same moment Daedalos moved from the gate, and the problem between them became smaller and then smaller and then done, the things retreating in a way that did not look like retreat so much as choosing somewhere else to be, dissolving back into the treeline and the predawn dark.

Daedalos stood breathing in the gateway for a moment. He looked at the four of them.

He looked at the twins.

He stepped aside.

---

Inside the village, the sky was beginning to lighten. A bird somewhere. The smell of wood smoke from the first fires being laid. The smell of the river still wrong, but less, or maybe that was only Ares's exhaustion rearranging his perceptions.

He got the twins to a doorstep. He made sure Mike was moving in the right direction. He registered that there were voices somewhere, that the village was starting to wake, that someone had seen them come in and someone else had been told.

And then the ground moved.

Not the ground. Him.

The exhaustion and the injuries and the hours in the dark, all of it arriving at once like a bill being presented, and he was on his knees before he had made any decision about it. And then he was not on his knees either.

---

He came to in his own home, in his own bed, in the midday light.

Mark was sitting beside him in the chair from the corner. He had fallen asleep in it, head to the side, with the particular restfulness of someone who had been keeping watch long enough that their body had made the choice for them. When Ares moved, Mark woke. Not smoothly. The way you wake when you have been waiting for something.

"How long," Ares said.

"Six hours, roughly." Mark rubbed his face. "You scared everyone. I want you to know that. For the historical record."

Ares was quiet for a moment. He looked at the ceiling.

"The twins," he said.

"Lesen hasn't left them. Peter's been in and out. They're both — Felan's talking. Vinie is sleeping but Peter says it's the right kind of sleeping." He paused. "Mike is at his father's house. His arm is going to take a few weeks."

"The water."

"Still wrong. Peter says it should start clearing. He doesn't know when." Mark looked at his hands. "He's been talking to people. About what you found in there. The thing that maintained it."

Ares did not say anything.

"It said it would come back," Mark said. He was not asking.

"Yes."

Mark sat with that for a moment. He was the kind of person who sat with things — who let reality be what it was without rushing to rearrange it. It was one of the things Ares had always found reliable about him.

"Then we have some time," he said. "Between now and when it does."

Outside, the village was going about the business of the day. The sounds of a place trying to put itself back together: something being repaired, a conversation happening at the edge of hearing, the creak of a gate.

Ares lay in his own bed and listened to Vinemore existing and thought about the crack in the floor of the old mine, and the energy moving through it from somewhere deep and slow and old, and the creature that had looked at him with decision rather than rage before it chose to leave.

*I will return. With help.*

He thought about what Felan had told him. About the places where this had already happened — the other wells in other areas, the pattern that had been running far longer than one village's water problems. About the wanderer who had given the last of what he had to two teenagers because there was no one else in reach.

He thought: *this is not over. This was only the beginning of understanding what it is.*

He lay there until he fell asleep again, which took less time than he expected.

---

*End of Chapter Two.*

## Chapter Three — The Slow Work of After

He woke in the dark.

Not from anything — just the body deciding it was done. The house was quiet. The village was quiet. Through the window the night sky had the particular clarity it gets after rain has spent itself, everything washed down to its actual components.

There was a folded piece of paper on the stool beside the bed. He did not remember it being there when he woke the first time. Mark must have left it, or Mike himself, before he went home.

He unfolded it.

The handwriting was large and slightly tilted, as if written quickly and in insufficient light:

*Went home. Father wanted to see the arm. You're going to be fine. — M*

He read it twice. Nothing had been left out. He folded it back up and set it on the stool and lay back and looked at the ceiling for a while.

Then he got up and walked through his own house in the dark — the kitchen, the back door, the yard. The air outside was cool and smelled of wet earth and the beginning of something different. He stood in it for a while, not thinking about anything in particular, just being in the body he had put through a night underground and letting it have a few minutes of the ordinary dark.

He went back inside. He lay down.

He slept.

---

Peter knocked at what Ares estimated, without opening his eyes, was early morning. The light behind his eyelids had the quality of something that had only recently decided to exist.

The doctor looked like he had already made three rounds and was running on the tea he'd had between them. He had the particular energy of a man who had been awake since before the problem started and was not going to stop until the problem was done.

"Ms. Kathy," he said. He read something in Ares's face — the unresolved thread — and held up a hand before anything could be asked. "She's awake. Weak, but past the worst." A pause. "She asked about you. The young man who knocked on her door."

He said it with the slight surprise of a man who had not connected the two things before.

He left to make his next call.

---

Ares made food slowly. His body had opinions about what the previous day had asked of it, and the food helped with some of them. He ate at the table in the morning light and looked at nothing in particular and thought about the order of things — who to see, in what sequence, what was owed and what was just wanted.

He had come back from the cave with more than he had brought. The question was what to do with the weight of it.

---

She answered the door herself. Slowly, one hand on the frame — but upright.

She looked him over in the way of someone cataloguing damage and finding the total acceptable. Then she moved aside to let him in without explaining that she was letting him in, which was the old woman's way of doing most things.

Her house smelled of dried herbs and old paper and the particular staleness of rooms closed too many days by someone too sick to open a window. She went to the chair by the window and sat in it with the deliberate satisfaction of someone reclaiming a thing they had missed.

She had a request. Simple and finite: the mushrooms she collected herself, from the east river bank. She knew exactly where they grew and what to look for. She could not go herself yet.

He went.

---

The east bank was starting to show the first signs of something changing. Not clean — not yet — but less wrong. A heron stood at the water's edge in the ordinary way herons stand, which was a small thing and not a small thing.

He found the mushrooms where she had said: in the damp ground near the water's edge, in a spread along the shadow of the bank, patient as they always were. He collected them carefully and came back.

---

She was where he had left her.

He set the mushrooms on the table. She looked at them with the particular satisfaction of someone whose knowledge of a thing has been confirmed by a person who followed their instructions correctly.

Then she looked at him.

"You are as kind as your father," she said. "The same way of being helpful without making it a performance. The same way of just doing it and then standing there."

She said it the way she said most things — as observation, not compliment.

He did not know what to say to it. He waited.

"He was a good man." She was looking at the mushrooms, but talking somewhere further away. "He could have stayed here. He had work, and people who liked him. There was a girl." The edge of something that might have been a laugh, years ago, and now was just the shape of one. "But he thought he should be somewhere useful. He had ideas about what useful meant. Said the capital needed good people more than the village did — that the village already had enough of them."

She was quiet for a moment.

"He went to Aethion. The Royal Guard." She looked up at Ares. "I heard he made it. That he is still there." A small lift of her shoulders — as much as she could manage. "I don't know more than that. I only know he left, and that he was the kind of man who finishes what he decides to do."

Ares sat with this.

A man in a city he had never been to, doing work he could only imagine, who had decided that the capital needed good people more than the village did. He had never been to Aethion. He had not had much reason to think about it.

He thanked her. She waved this away with the practiced efficiency of someone who has decided they are owed things and does not need gratitude added on top.

"Come back when these run out," she said. "You know where they grow now."

---

Lesen answered the door before he finished knocking.

She was a woman who had been wrung out and was standing with what was left. The shadows under her eyes were the accumulation of days. But her grip on the door frame was not the grip of someone who needed it to stand — it was the grip of someone who had made a decision about standing and was committed to it.

She looked at Ares for a moment. Then she stepped back and let him in. Then she said something quietly that he was going to carry for a long time.

Felan was on the settee, the kind of awake where the body has voted for horizontal and the mind refuses to comply. He looked significantly better than he had in the safe room — color, focus, something returned to his face. When he saw Ares he sat up fully and appeared to immediately begin thinking about the crack in the ceiling of the Spider Queen's chamber.

"Not today," Ares said.

Felan considered arguing. He decided against it. "No," he agreed. "Not today."

Vinie was in the back room. Through the doorway: the quiet even sound of sleep that is the right kind.

---

Mr. Gragi opened the door and this time did not ask if Ares was a doctor.

He stepped back. That was all. The door, and the step back.

Derol was in the main room — sitting, pale, moving with care, but sitting. He shook Ares's hand with the gravity of a man who has had several days lying down to think about what things are worth. He asked about the cave. Ares told him what he could. Derol listened with his whole attention and then said, in the particular voice of a man who has spent his whole life near water: "It will clear." He said it as if it were a matter he had personally decided.

From somewhere in the back of the house, Fredy appeared, looked at Ares with large serious eyes for exactly three seconds, and disappeared again.

---

Dario opened the door and there was nothing defensive about it anymore.

Miria was on the settee, color returning to her face. They sat for a while and talked about nothing much — the weather since the storm, the roof three houses over that was being repaired, the way the village was moving back toward itself. The pregnancy was no longer a weight she was carrying alone; she had told people now, or the people who mattered. She sat with both hands on her stomach in the absent, unselfconscious way of women who have stopped treating something as a secret.

When Ares stood to leave, Dario shook his hand at the door.

"Thank you," he said. Two words. The right two.

---

Nathan's forge was already going when Ares arrived.

He looked up from the work when Ares came in. He looked at the hand axe at Ares's hip — the borrowed one, the one he had handed over with *this sounds like it matters more* and the reasonable expectation it would come back.

Ares unhooked it from his belt and held it out.

Nathan looked at the axe. He looked at Ares. He set his tongs on the bench and crossed his arms.

"Keep it," he said.

Ares looked at the axe. He thought about the way it had felt when he first picked it up in this forge — the specific weight of it, the way he had thought: *yes. This.* He thought about what it had done in the dark and what it had cost to do it.

"Are you sure," he said.

"I wasn't going to take it back from you," Nathan said. "I just wanted to see how you'd ask."

He picked up his tongs and went back to the work.

---

Leman's house was the last one.

A woman he recognized — Leman's wife, a woman with the look of someone who has not slept properly in four days and has made peace with it — opened the door. From the back room, through an interior door, the sound of low voices: Leman talking, another person responding. Awake. Speaking.

She stepped outside and pulled the door almost shut behind her. She looked at Ares.

She did not say anything. She went inside for a moment. She came back with a packet of rations — wrapped, clean, the kind prepared for a person going somewhere. She held it out.

He took it.

She went back inside. The door closed.

He stood on the step for a moment with the rations in his hands, listening to the sound of a house putting itself back together around a man who was getting better.

---

The day was ending the way days end after a great deal has happened — not with ceremony, just with the light getting longer and lower and the village settling into its evening sounds.

He walked back through Vinemore with the axe on his hip and the rations in his pack and the particular tiredness of a day that had been necessary. The water still ran slightly wrong from the taps — less than before, measurably less, but still not right. The Spider Queen was still somewhere in the rock above the river cave, alive, retreated. The crack in the mine floor was still open. The well was still there. The creature was out in the world going toward whatever help it had promised itself.

He had done everything here that could be done here.

He thought about what Felan had told them in the safe room. The pattern of the wells. The places that had already been taken — somewhere east, somewhere south, a network of small disasters no one had connected because no one had yet been in enough of them to see the shape. He thought about the wanderer, badly wounded, giving the last of what he had because he was running out and still needed the work to continue.

He thought about a man in Aethion who had decided the capital needed good people more than the village did.

He did not know what was out there. He had one zone behind him and a world ahead, and he understood now — in the quiet, bone-deep way of things that settle rather than arrive — that the cave had not been an ending. It had been an introduction. To the scope of what he was now part of. To the size of the problem and the smallness of any one person's capacity to address it and the fact that this was apparently not going to stop him.

The village would be here when he needed to come back. It would always be here.

He went home. He ate. He slept.

Tomorrow he would start thinking about east.

---

*End of Chapter Three.*
